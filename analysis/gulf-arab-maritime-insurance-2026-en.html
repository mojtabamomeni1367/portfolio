Marine Insurance After the 40-Day War: The Hidden Cost of Bypassing Hormuz

Alternative pipelines and ports can ease disruption at Hormuz. Usable capacity, voyage costs and delivery reliability determine their commercial value.

Changing the loading port can preserve oil exports, but it raises a question for producers and buyers. Can a cargo reach its destination on time, at an acceptable cost and with valid cover? On November 3, the Associated Press reported that Saudi Aramco had used its East-West pipeline and the port of Yanbu to sustain a significant share of its exports despite disruptions to tanker traffic through Hormuz. The experience shows the value of alternative infrastructure. Its benefits, however, depend on port capacity, conditions along the sea route and the cost of insurance.

Marine insurance is part of this calculation for Arab countries on the Persian Gulf. Oil that reaches a terminal still needs to be loaded and shipped to a buyer. Moreover, capacity, delays and contractual terms can affect each stage. Assessing whether energy exports can withstand disruption therefore requires three questions. How much cargo can the alternative route absorb? On what terms can a ship sail? How reliably can the buyer expect delivery?

Marine Insurance and the Real Capacity of Alternative Routes

The US Energy Information Administration reports that Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates can move some crude to ports outside the Strait of Hormuz. The Saudi pipeline reaches Yanbu on the Red Sea, while Abu Dhabi's pipeline takes crude to Fujairah on the Gulf of Oman. The routes give the two exporters different options. Nevertheless, a pipeline's existence does not determine how much oil can be exported in a particular week. Actual capacity depends on the grade of oil, equipment, storage, loading schedules and the ships available.

In this calculation, insurance cover helps determine whether the route is commercially usable. The port's distance from Hormuz is one variable. If the ship must subsequently pass through high-risk waters, higher voyage costs may reduce part of the advantage gained on land. Similarly, rerouting a cargo of crude does not necessarily create a route for liquefied natural gas or other products. A single number for capacity that bypasses Hormuz can therefore obscure important differences between commodities and the facilities that handle them.

An alternative route is more valuable when it can operate over several weeks. A trial shipment establishes that a route was usable at a particular time. However, sustaining exports requires moving additional cargoes without lengthy queues, pressure on storage or disruption to buyers' schedules. Consequently, the number of repeat loadings and the time ships spend waiting can give a clearer picture of usable capacity than the pipeline's stated capacity alone. These measures also help separate a short surge from a sustained change in trade.

Marine Insurance and the Cost of Voyage Risk

Shipping companies assess a set of costs and obligations before deciding whether to undertake a voyage. The duration and geographic scope of cover, exclusions and claims arrangements can affect whether a vessel is dispatched. Even if an insurer quotes a rate for a route, the terms may vary across vessels and cargo owners. Therefore, a headline premium cannot explain the cost or feasibility of every voyage. An assessment also needs to distinguish a quotation from cover that an operator can secure for the intended sailing.

Reuters reported in September that higher war-risk cover costs had affected Yanbu, illustrating that Red Sea ports can also face higher costs. That report described conditions at the time. To assess a November voyage, however, a buyer needs the quotation date, destination, vessel and contractual terms. Marine insurance is also only one cost. Freight, waiting time and detours affect the total. Consequently, savings in one part of a voyage may be offset by higher costs elsewhere and may change which terminal is commercially preferable.

Crew safety is a separate consideration. A contract can cover some financial losses, but the decision to sail also depends on the risks to people and the availability of emergency assistance. Thus, insurance availability alone does not establish that normal operations have resumed. To assess the stability of trade, it is necessary to see whether shipowners, crews, ports and buyers can agree on a workable voyage and repeat it.

Marine Insurance and the Buyer's Confidence in Delivery

Exporters in the region have different alternatives. For a producer that can move crude to two coasts, selecting a port is part of managing supply. In contrast, rerouting is harder for an exporter whose product requires a specific terminal or specialized vessel. A policy that helps a crude exporter therefore may work differently for a gas exporter or a neighboring country's importers. The same disruption can affect their ability to meet delivery commitments in different ways.

Accordingly, marine insurance should be evaluated alongside delivery schedules and the ability to replace supplies. A buyer unsure when a cargo will arrive might make another purchase or seek greater flexibility in the contract. Both choices have financial implications for the exporter, even if the original cargo eventually sails. Publishing loading data, ship waiting times and the continuity of voyages can help buyers determine which routes can be used consistently. Moreover, data collected over time can show whether delays are concentrated at a terminal or occur later in the voyage.

Arab countries on the Persian Gulf can reduce some of the pressure from disruption at Hormuz by diversifying ports and routes. The result, however, depends on performance. Marine insurance signals sustained improvement when cover is offered on workable terms, ships repeat voyages and buyers receive cargoes on schedule. If any of those conditions remains weak, moving oil to a different port may solve part of the problem while shifting costs and delays to the next stage. A buyer judging reliability will look at the time from the commitment to delivery, including any changes in route or terms along the way.
