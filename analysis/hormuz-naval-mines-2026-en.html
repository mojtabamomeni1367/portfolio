Naval Mines in the Strait of Hormuz: Is the War at Sea Changing?

Conflicting reports about three vessels raise questions about the limits of U.S. escorts and the effects of a persistent hazard on insurance and oil trade.

Reports of naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz raise a question that goes beyond the fate of three tankers. Could the threat to commercial vessels persist between attacks and turn sporadic disruption into a lasting restriction on trade? Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy said three oil tankers struck mines and caught fire despite U.S. air cover. UK Maritime Trade Operations reported that unknown projectiles hit three eastbound vessels on November 23. Its warning, issued the following day, said all three vessels continued their voyages and all crew were accounted for. The extent of damage and any environmental impact remained unknown. Associated Press · UKMTO warning

What the tanker reports establish

The UK warning confirms an incident involving three vessels. It leaves the weapons unidentified and does not substantiate the Iranian account of mine strikes and major fires. The timing and the number of vessels are insufficient to match the accounts ship by ship. The distinction matters because maritime businesses use different assumptions when responding to an isolated strike and when assessing a continuing hazard to future voyages.

The latest reports follow a series of earlier incidents. In an update on November 22, the Joint Maritime Information Center recorded three strikes by unknown projectiles on November 20 and 21. It said seven tankers had been struck since November 16, as of the time of its update, and that earlier Iranian claims of mine strikes remained unconfirmed. The record shows continuing attacks on shipping. It leaves open which vessels were escorted and whether the incidents shared a common cause. JMIC update, November 22

Could the mine threat create a lasting restriction?

After a direct attack, a shipping company can reassess its next departure against the latest conditions. A confirmed mine hazard would extend the time horizon of that decision. Even during a period with no new incident, owners would still need a credible assessment of whether the underlying risk had changed. Confidence would depend on the authorities’ assessment and response, followed by enough successful crossings for companies to judge that conditions had improved.

Disruption can persist without giving either party dependable control of the strait. To establish that one side can impose passage conditions over time, the public record would have to show sustained changes in which ships move, which cargoes are delayed or canceled, and how operators respond to restrictions. An intermediate outcome is entirely possible. Some ships could continue to transit while longer waits, higher freight charges and tighter insurance reduce effective commercial capacity. A waterway can remain formally open while becoming too unreliable for some scheduled cargoes.

What can U.S. escorts achieve?

Air cover and naval escorts can reduce some risks from direct attacks. They can also improve communication and speed up the initial response to incidents. A potential mine hazard calls for its own assessment. An escorted crossing demonstrates that a specific ship made it through under specific conditions. Other operators still need information they can use to plan their next departures. At the policy level, the value of the U.S. response will depend on coordination with maritime partners, credible warnings, action to address verified hazards and the continuity of support for commercial traffic.

Success will look different in Washington and in a shipping office. A completed voyage can demonstrate that passage remains possible. An owner or charterer needs to quote a price, arrange cover and commit to a delivery date. If escorts allow selected ships to cross while other operators keep postponing departures, the immediate security effort may achieve a limited objective while trade remains impaired. The commercial test is whether enough ordinary voyages become predictable again.

How marine insurance turns risk into cost

The decision to sail involves the vessel owner, the charterer, the cargo owner, insurers and sometimes lenders. Each participant can apply a different threshold for accepting risk. An increase in war-risk premiums is one visible consequence. Stricter conditions, quotations that expire sooner and the postponement of voyages can carry substantial costs even when coverage remains available. Waiting ties up vessels, interrupts loading schedules and makes it harder for buyers to plan deliveries.

Changes in coverage terms over successive quotations can reveal whether insurers expect the danger to persist. Several successful transits may give operators some confidence while insurers still require tighter terms. If new voyages are booked alongside an improvement in coverage, there is stronger evidence of a recovery. These links between coverage, freight and scheduling are explored in the companion marine insurance analysis.

Which indicators matter for the oil market?

The U.S. Energy Information Administration estimates that oil flows through Hormuz averaged 20.9 million barrels per day in the first half of 2025. That historical figure explains the strait’s importance. Measuring the impact in November 2026 requires contemporary data. The first signs of disruption may appear in tanker rates or in the difference between delivered prices in two markets. If delays persist, refineries may draw on stocks or pay more for replacement cargoes. The broader price response will depend on how long deliveries are affected and how much substitute supply is available. U.S. Energy Information Administration

JMIC reported very low levels of commercial traffic visible in automatic identification data in its November 22 update. Tracking counts can diverge from estimates compiled through other channels. A stronger measure of disruption would combine observed crossings with loading data, arrivals at destination ports and changes in voyage schedules. If several measures deteriorate together, there is more reason to expect an impact on actual deliveries. JMIC update, November 22

The outlook

As of November 24, the public record shows a continuing threat to merchant shipping. The British warning records hits on three vessels and says they continued their voyages. The claim that the vessels struck mines still awaits independent confirmation. If credible assessments establish a persistent hazard and traffic and insurance remain constrained, the disruption could outlast individual incidents. The test for the U.S. response is whether it can help restore reliable, repeatable passage for commercial shipping at scale.
