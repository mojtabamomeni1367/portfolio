Iran-US negotiations on 3 fronts: Diplomacy under military threat and Washington’s credibility crisis

Simultaneous diplomatic messages, warnings of renewed strikes and intensified economic pressure have placed Iran-US negotiations before a decisive test. Washington is attempting to combine these instruments to change Tehran’s calculations. Yet the continuing conflict and its economic consequences are also raising costs for the United States and its partners, while casting doubt on promises that military action would quickly achieve Washington’s objectives.

JD Vance’s letter to Mohsen Rezaei, the return of Qatari mediators and Tom Barrack’s optimism about de-escalation indicate that diplomatic channels remain active. Reports about possible strikes after the US midterm elections meanwhile show that military action remains under consideration. The central question extends beyond the existence of parallel tracks: can military pressure produce an implementable agreement, or does it deepen distrust and restrict the space for compromise? Their interaction matters more than the mere fact that Washington continues to pursue both options.

Iran-US negotiations: The distance between a message and an agreement

Published accounts differ over the purpose of Vance’s letter. Media reports have linked it to efforts to reopen substantive talks, while Donald Trump has described the contact as an initiative to help provide medical supplies. That discrepancy complicates assessments of the diplomatic opening’s scope. Nevertheless, contact at this level matters because Washington still considers communication with Tehran necessary. Pressure has not removed the need for a political agreement to end the crisis. Nor does confirming that a message was delivered establish that either side has accepted a negotiating framework.

Barrack’s comments require similar precision. He described the parties as close to an understanding on a pause in hostilities and de-escalation, rather than announcing a comprehensive settlement. In Iran-US negotiations, a temporary pause could create an opening for addressing the main disputes, but cannot substitute for resolving them. The return of Qatari mediators likewise indicates continuing efforts to narrow differences. Their success should be measured through changes in positions and commitments, rather than the frequency of diplomatic visits or optimistic public statements by those involved in the process.

Iranian Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, meanwhile, has made progress conditional on acceptance of Iran’s seven conditions. Their complete details are not clear from the reporting reviewed, although ending the port blockade, easing oil restrictions and accessing frozen assets feature among Tehran’s stated demands. The sequence of implementation remains crucial. If Iran relinquishes leverage before receiving tangible concessions, what guarantees would ensure that Washington fulfils its obligations? Without a credible answer, diplomatic optimism encounters an implementation problem. Each side’s demands must be connected to identifiable reciprocal steps, rather than promises deferred indefinitely.

Iran-US negotiations under the pressure of military threats

Reports concerning potential attacks after the midterms do not establish that a final decision to resume strikes has been taken. Nevertheless, linking military timing to the American political calendar raises concerns about domestic calculations shaping the crisis. Threats can support diplomacy when they serve a limited objective and leave a clear route away from confrontation. If Tehran believes negotiations merely provide time to prepare another attack, its incentive to make concessions will diminish. Mediation would then face greater resistance, regardless of how intensively intermediaries work to preserve contact between the parties.

The dismissal of Lieutenant General Brett Sylvia, the Joint Staff’s senior official for strategy, plans and policy, has added uncertainty. The Associated Press reported Pentagon accusations that he disclosed sensitive information, allegations that should not be confused with a judicial finding. The Pentagon also attributed central operational planning for the Iran conflict to US Central Command. His removal therefore cannot, without supporting evidence, be treated as proof that he opposed renewed strikes. Its significance lies in a senior personnel change while military options are being considered, rather than establishing an undocumented policy dispute.

Beyond personnel changes, Iran-US negotiations highlight the difficulty of converting military power into political results. The ability to inflict damage is different from the ability to impose a durable settlement. Continuing reliance on mediation and unresolved core disputes indicate that Washington’s preferred end state has not been secured. In this sense, the image of overwhelming American military authority has suffered: force alone has not resolved the confrontation. That assessment concerns strategic effectiveness and should not be confused with claiming that American combat capabilities have disappeared or that further operations are impossible.

Iran-US negotiations: Economic pressure and the global costs of war

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has described the pressure campaign’s objective as severing the Iranian government’s economic lifelines. This approach assumes that increasing the cost of resistance will compel Tehran to change its position. Economic hardship and falling revenue, however, do not automatically produce Washington’s preferred political decision. Intensified pressure could instead strengthen demands for guarantees and compensation. Sanctions become a negotiating instrument when a clear, credible route to their removal also exists. Otherwise, Tehran may see little reason to exchange leverage for relief that could later be withdrawn.

The consequences of the US-Israeli war against Iran have also extended beyond Iran’s borders. The International Monetary Fund has described disrupted energy production and trade routes, higher food and energy prices, and increased uncertainty. The impact varies across countries, with some economies adapting more effectively. Nevertheless, spreading costs confront Washington’s partners with a political question: why absorb the consequences of a strategy that has yet to produce a stable outcome? This gap exposes American international standing to erosion, particularly when military escalation imposes additional burdens on countries whose cooperation Washington needs.

Ultimately, Iran-US negotiations will escape the cycle of threats and interruptions only if pressure is accompanied by concrete concessions, verifiable commitments and a credible prospect of ending the war. Continued conflict without securing stated objectives generates economic costs and weakens Washington’s claim to manage regional order. Diplomacy and deterrence can reinforce each other, but open-ended threats erode the trust an agreement requires. The White House’s central test is turning power into sustainable arrangements, something military operations alone have so far failed to accomplish.
