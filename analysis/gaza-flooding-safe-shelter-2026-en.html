Flooding in Gaza: Why Are Thousands of Families Still Without Safe Shelter?

Flooding in Gaza exposed gaps from aid approvals to delivery. Follow the path to safe shelter through drainage, transport and access to essential services.

Flooding in Gaza has exposed the distance between allowing aid into the territory and getting it to families. In its situation report for November 8, published the following day, the United Nations humanitarian coordination office described flooded displacement sites after heavy rain and wind. A preliminary estimate cited in the report put the toll at more than 6,000 family shelters destroyed and around 17,400 others damaged. However, those figures may change as assessments continue. The practical question is what has to happen before a family with a damaged tent can move somewhere safe.

Flooding in Gaza and the distance between approval and delivery

Aid organizations must first be able to bring in the items they need. The UN reported a need for more Israeli approvals for shelter supplies, spare parts, engine oil and heavy machinery. It also identified a funding shortage. Meanwhile, Israeli officials said they had facilitated the entry of tents and winter supplies. Comparing these accounts requires knowing which goods they cover, over what period and where deliveries went. A shipment admitted earlier can be relevant to that assessment without showing whether supplies match current needs.

After the rains, the number of trucks is one indicator of access. However, a replacement tent alone cannot drain a camp, and drainage equipment needs fuel, working parts, operators and a passable route. Therefore, looking at shipments by purpose gives a clearer picture than adding every item to one total. In some locations, the most urgent need could be a water pump, while in others it could be a usable place to set up a tent.

It is also useful to distinguish approval, entry and delivery. An approved item can still wait for inspection or transport. Similarly, a truck entering the territory may have yet to reach a warehouse or its intended destination. To assess the response to flooding in Gaza, the time between a request and delivery and the number of families receiving the required items give more direct evidence than the number of approvals alone.

Safe shelter starts with the site

Flooding in Gaza showed why replacing a tent in the same spot may leave a family exposed. If a camp lies in the path of runoff or the ground cannot drain rainfall, the new tent may flood again. Consequently, clearing drainage routes, improving ground conditions and checking the safety of nearby structures belong in a shelter response. The appropriate mix depends on what created the risk in each location.

However, moving families carries costs. A replacement site needs water, sanitation, health care and reliable access routes. For instance, moving to higher ground can make it harder for older residents or people with disabilities to reach a clinic. Decisions about relocation should therefore involve the families affected and take into account the risks and services at both locations.

Meanwhile, smaller works can take effect sooner than building a new site. The UN report described emergency work to improve ground conditions at some camps and identified separate needs for drainage, lighting and latrine repair. After flooding in Gaza, assessing sites separately can show where drainage and ground work may be enough and where families may need another location. The distinction affects which equipment, workers and vehicles responders send.

Distribution also faces local constraints. A broken vehicle, obstructed road or shortage of workers can delay deliveries after supplies enter. Therefore, it helps to record the cause of delays in each area. If the main obstacle is an inaccessible road, increasing tent stocks in a warehouse will do little to shorten the wait. Conversely, clearing a route will have limited value when the required items are still unavailable.

How should the response to flooding in Gaza be assessed?

First, measure time. Regular reporting should show how long a family waits after its shelter need is registered. An overall average may obscure much longer delays at particular sites. Therefore, separating delivery times by location and type of need would make it easier to prioritize the next shipment. For example, a site where families with young children lack drinking water and dry shelter may need faster action than one awaiting materials for planned repairs.

Second, measure whether aid can be used. A tent can provide shelter when it is installed on safe ground and a family has access to essential services. Moreover, visiting a site after the next rainfall can show whether improvements to the ground and drainage worked. That visit also helps responders decide which measures could work elsewhere and which sites still need attention.

Third, measure whether families can report problems. A household missing from a distribution list or receiving unsuitable supplies needs a way to flag the issue. Recording complaints can therefore help aid teams distinguish a supply shortage from a distribution error or a transport problem. Without such records, delivery figures may suggest that needs were met even when some families remain without essential items.

These measures are related. For instance, a rapid delivery can still leave people at risk if the shelter cannot be set up safely. Similarly, a site improvement can have limited effect if families cannot reach it or services are unavailable there. Reporting these stages together would make it easier to see why two sites receiving similar quantities of aid can have different outcomes.

Ultimately, the impact of flooding in Gaza can be assessed at household level. Can residents spend the night somewhere dry, reach water and sanitation, and stay in place when rain returns? The answers depend on approvals as well as transport, installation and maintenance. Consequently, following assistance from the crossing to the shelter would make responsibilities clearer and show which decisions had the greatest effect on families. It would also help responders identify needs that remain unmet before the next storm makes them more urgent.
