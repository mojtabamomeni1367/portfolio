Eisenkot government: What would a coalition with Lieberman do on Iran?

Eisenkot and Lieberman’s pact has not formed a government; coalition arithmetic, the October 7 inquiry vote and an unsubstantiated Iran claim show the limits on policy change.

As a first step, the November 24 coalition agreement between Gadi Eisenkot’s Yashar party and Avigdor Lieberman’s Yisrael Beiteinu was the first formal pact in Israel’s effort to form a government after October’s election. However, it should not be mistaken for a new government or agreement on every policy.

Meanwhile, these developments coincided with caretaker Defense Minister Israel Katz’s claim that Iran was developing chemical weapons. Katz said the program was advancing, while also asserting that Iran did not yet have the ability to attack Israel with such weapons. No public evidence accompanied the assessment. A European diplomat told The Times of Israel that there was no clear intelligence of an imminent capability, although there were weak signs of activity. Therefore, the question about an Eisenkot government is not simply whether its tone would differ from Netanyahu’s. The more important issue is what limits a possible cabinet would place on the timing and scope of military action.

The Eisenkot government and the coalition arithmetic

For context, the October 27 election gave neither bloc the 61 seats needed for a Knesset majority. Reuters reported that Eisenkot’s bloc holds 48 seats and Netanyahu’s alliance 53, while Arab parties won 19 seats collectively. Eisenkot has until December 6 to form a government, with a possible two-week extension. The Lieberman agreement is a step, but it does not create a majority. Moreover, the Democrats’ pause could make negotiations harder.

However, these numbers explain why every available path carries a political cost. Eisenkot’s bloc needs Arab-party support, yet Lieberman and some other partners have opposed their participation in a cabinet. Another option is an arrangement with Likud or religious parties, which Golan considers inconsistent with the pledge to replace Netanyahu. Consequently, any deal could affect domestic policy, military service, institutional reform and the government’s priorities in wartime.

In addition, the Knesset’s preliminary vote on an inquiry also makes the coalition question about more than the distribution of ministries. The 65-to-50 vote was the first of four steps needed to pass a law; it did not launch a full inquiry. Still, it showed that temporary cooperation between Eisenkot’s bloc and Arab parties can change the parliamentary agenda. That cooperation does not guarantee a future government, but it demonstrates the parties’ potential influence.

Still, if formed, an Eisenkot government would face a basic reality: agreement on replacing Netanyahu does not automatically mean agreement on Iran, Gaza or Lebanon. Parties may unite to change leadership while disagreeing about military force, relations with Washington and accountability after the war. Thus, seat totals are only the starting point. Cabinet cohesion and its ability to survive difficult votes will determine whether a promise of change becomes durable policy.

Iran: A different timetable or a different goal?

By contrast, on Iran, both Eisenkot and Netanyahu have expressed concern about Tehran’s military programs. The difference that has emerged recently concerns more the urgency of the threat and the timing of action. The Washington Post reported Eisenkot saying he saw no direct and immediate threat that required Israel to act before a new government was formed. Netanyahu accused him of downplaying the danger. That disagreement matters, but it is not proof that Eisenkot supports a conciliatory strategy.

Therefore, for an Eisenkot government, practical options could include continued deterrence, close coordination with the United States, diplomatic pressure and military readiness. In addition, the recent war may change calculations about the cost of a pre-emptive strike. Such an operation could target specific capabilities, but it could also invite retaliation, disrupt energy markets and widen the conflict.

However, Katz’s chemical-weapons claim needs particular caution. By November 25, no publicly assessable evidence had been released, and available reports did not independently detail the stage of any program. The diplomat’s remarks neither conclusively confirmed nor fully rejected the allegation: the source cited a lack of clear information about an imminent threat and weak signs of activity.

More importantly, judging an Eisenkot government requires comparing statements with observable decisions. Would it publish an intelligence assessment or submit it to independent review? Would it make military action conditional on a specific and near-term threat? Would it establish mechanisms to coordinate with Washington and reduce miscalculation? Those questions are more useful than labels such as “hawk” or “moderate.” Eisenkot’s military background, by itself, does not answer them.

From a change of government to a change in conduct

At the same time, a new prime minister could change the style of communication with Washington, coalition management and the presentation of intelligence. Yet a fundamental shift in Iran policy would require agreement within the cabinet and the ability to sustain it. If a government relied on Arab parties from outside the coalition, disputes over Gaza and their political status could make that support fragile.

For comparison, this analysis connects to the site’s earlier coverage of Israel’s election and the role of Arab parties in the Knesset balance. That story showed that Arab parliamentary support could influence the choice of prime minister. This week’s developments test whether that support can become sustained cooperation on legislation and government formation, or remains limited to selected votes.

More specifically, on Iran, a real change would be visible in decision criteria, not only rhetoric: transparency about intelligence, clear thresholds for action, coordination with allies and preparations to contain retaliation. Conversely, if both leaders define the threat similarly but disagree about when to respond, the difference may be managerial rather than strategic.

Ultimately, the central question therefore has no immediate answer. A new government might use less personalized rhetoric and more collective decision-making, yet that would not guarantee less pressure on Tehran or reduced military readiness. The final coalition, its formal security commitments and its first decisions after taking office will provide stronger evidence.

Ultimately, the Eisenkot government will be judged by its first security decisions.

Those early choices will clarify what the Eisenkot government means by security.
