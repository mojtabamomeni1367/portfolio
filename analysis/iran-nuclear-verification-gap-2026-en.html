Nuclear Verification in Iran After the War Depends on 3 Conditions

A credible inventory, a workable sequence of commitments and continuous monitoring would make a nuclear agreement with Iran easier to verify.

An agreement signed after a war needs a measurable starting point. In Iran's case, that starting point is disputed. In remarks reported by the Associated Press in late October, International Atomic Energy Agency Director General Rafael Grossi said the agency had identified no activity to repair or reconstruct damaged facilities. However, the same report noted that the agency had been unable to verify the status of Iran's highly enriched uranium stockpile. These statements address two different questions. One concerns the state of buildings and equipment. The other concerns accounting for the material itself.

Under these conditions, nuclear verification can narrow the gap between a political promise and measurable action. The contacts Reuters reported on November 3 had yet to yield an agreement. If maritime de-escalation or a halt to attacks comes before nuclear talks, the parties need to know what information will be preserved during the transition and who will have access to it for an independent review. Otherwise, a technical uncertainty could become a political dispute on the first day of implementation.

Nuclear Verification and a Credible Baseline

The first requirement is an inventory and a clear account of starting conditions. The last figure obtained before facilities were damaged describes a particular date. Between inspections, material might have been moved, remained at a damaged site, or been recorded differently in new documents. Determining which account fits the evidence requires declarations, transfer records, sampling and appropriate access. Each supplies a piece of the picture, and confidence depends on whether those pieces fit together.

Accordingly, nuclear verification cannot end with publication of a new number. If inspectors can reach only one site, their findings can be precise about that location while leaving questions elsewhere. Moreover, gaps in historical records may take time to reconstruct. An agreement can recognize this by separating confirmed findings, provisional estimates and unresolved items. That distinction gives both sides an opportunity to correct errors before they become disputes and makes it harder to claim success prematurely.

Satellite imagery has a specific role in that process. Changes visible at a building can reveal signs of construction or traffic. However, imagery alone cannot establish the amount or composition of material inside a container. Conversely, an inspection without historical records may reveal conditions only on the day of the visit. Comparing several independent sources therefore limits the risk that a single image or official statement becomes the basis for a political decision. It also gives inspectors a way to explain why their confidence differs from one finding to another.

Nuclear Verification and the Order of Commitments

The second requirement is a workable sequence. Tehran may seek reassurance that broader access will bring an economic benefit. Washington, meanwhile, may want to see independently confirmed measures before offering relief. If each side expects the other to fulfill every obligation first, agreement on the end goal does little to resolve the impasse. A modest, specific sequence, such as exchanging initial records alongside a defined economic step, can reduce the risk of moving first.

In practice, nuclear monitoring needs feasible deadlines and clear responsibilities. Before implementation, parties should understand what evidence counts, who confirms receipt, and what happens if safety conditions delay access. A technical delay can reflect hazards at a damaged facility. However, repeated delays without explanation can erode confidence in implementation. A shared timetable and evidence make it easier to distinguish these situations. The distinction matters because the political response to a temporary safety delay may be different from the response to an indefinite denial of access.

The agency's duties and the negotiators' responsibilities should also remain distinct. Inspectors can report on material, facilities and the limits of access. Governments make decisions about lifting economic restrictions, suspending military operations and responding to disagreements. If every technical finding automatically triggers political consequences, pressure on the inspectors' work increases. A dispute process can instead allow time for an explanation and further examination before either side considers a retaliatory step. This arrangement also gives governments a way to raise a concern without turning every disagreement into a test of resolve.

Nuclear Verification After the First Report

The third requirement is continuity. A successful visit can reduce some uncertainty, but a durable agreement relies on regular reporting, preservation of records and a way to examine new discrepancies. If security conditions change, the parties need to know in advance what information can still be gathered and how access will resume. Consequently, temporary disruption can be distinguished from a prolonged interruption in monitoring. Keeping records available during a pause also reduces the amount of work inspectors must repeat once access returns.

In this setting, nuclear verification can give decision makers some distance from the pressure of daily headlines. Carefully worded reports that identify the limits of each finding help temper unrealistic expectations of inspectors. Furthermore, phased concessions allow new evidence to inform the next stage of implementation. That flexibility is useful when obligations are measurable and both sides apply the same criteria for success. Otherwise, a change of political position could be presented as a dispute over what the agreement originally required.

Negotiators face a practical question. Once the first commitment is fulfilled, what information will be available that cannot be obtained now, and who will confirm it? A specific answer turns nuclear verification from a general clause into a process for which governments can be held accountable. Clearer baselines and dispute procedures make it less likely that uncertainty about material or a delay in access will become grounds for renewed military pressure. Moreover, a first report with clearly identified gaps gives negotiators something concrete to address at the next meeting, even if it cannot resolve every question immediately.
