Iran's Nuclear Doctrine on November 21: Domestic Debate, China and Russia

A debate over Iran's nuclear posture is growing, even as Tehran says its official policy is unchanged and the meaning of China's and Russia's positions remains open to interpretation.

Iran's nuclear doctrine returned to headlines this week as senior officials acknowledged debate, though Tehran announced no formal change. In an interview published on November 21, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said arguments for revising the approach had “strong logic,” while also insisting that Iran's official position had not changed. His description of China and Russia's views was less clear. Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty noted that it was uncertain whether he meant the two countries oppose a change in Iranian policy or hold opposing views from one another. Therefore, the report establishes neither a joint objection by Beijing and Moscow nor a final Iranian decision.

Meanwhile, the debate unfolds amid military pressure, questions about safeguards, and a diplomatic impasse over enriched uranium. Araghchi said the stockpile would not be transferred abroad, but left room for a formula. RFE/RL cited the International Atomic Energy Agency's estimate that Iran had 440.9 kilograms of uranium enriched to 60 percent before the agency lost the ability to fully account for it. However, that figure alone does not prove a decision to build a weapon. Limited verification makes the material's status and any future agreement harder to assess. A previous analysis on this site, Iran's nuclear verification gap, examined the same divide between political assurances and verifiable information.

Iran's nuclear doctrine: Debate or decision?

First, public discussion of an option should not be confused with its formal adoption. Some Iranian politicians and security officials argue that NPT membership and IAEA monitoring did not prevent attacks on nuclear facilities. However, the argument reflects a security grievance after strikes; it does not establish that Tehran has decided to pursue a weapon. Araghchi's statement that official policy remains unchanged is therefore central, not incidental.

Moreover, raising an option can test foreign reactions, affect negotiations, or reveal internal disagreement. Still, without evidence of a formal decision, it is not possible to identify one motive as definitive. Even so, public discussion carries costs: governments may assign greater likelihood to a shift, while neighbors and markets reassess proliferation risks.

A formal change could affect Iran's NPT obligations, complicate IAEA cooperation, and bring sanctions or diplomatic isolation. Conversely, advocates may see it as stronger deterrence. The calculation is whether that benefit outweighs economic and military risks. Reporting does not settle the question, but public commentary is not a state decision. Any assessment of Iran's nuclear doctrine must distinguish discussion from policy.

Iran's nuclear doctrine and Beijing and Moscow

Araghchi's words about China and Russia require the same care. RFE/RL said the phrasing did not make clear whether both governments oppose a change in Tehran's policy or whether their views differ from each other. As a result, headlines presenting a definite joint Chinese-Russian veto go beyond what the reported quotation establishes. Their positions should be assessed through official statements, votes in international bodies, and practical conduct, rather than one ambiguous sentence.

Still, Beijing and Moscow have reasons to discourage a wider nuclear crisis, since both would bear costs from regional war and disrupted trade. China depends on stable energy imports, while Russia has little apparent interest in direct Iran-US confrontation. However, cooperation with Tehran does not mean either partner will endorse every Iranian choice; governments may disagree on one issue and cooperate on another.

Meanwhile, for Tehran, relations with the two powers are also multidimensional. Diplomatic backing and economic ties can soften some Western pressure, but they cannot substitute for verifiable arrangements or resolve the dispute with Washington. If the debate approaches a formal decision, Iran will have to weigh the consequences not only for ties with the United States and Europe, but also for eastern partners and neighboring governments. In this sense, Iran's nuclear doctrine has become a test both of strategic autonomy and of the limits of diplomatic support.

Iran's nuclear doctrine and the uranium-stockpile impasse

In practice, the obstacle to an agreement is the stockpile and whether it can be verified. Meanwhile, Iran says the material will remain inside the country; Washington seeks limits that can be measured. For example, possible options include dilution, conversion into less sensitive forms, restrictions on enrichment levels, seals and inventories, or phased monitoring. However, none is an agreement by itself. Each would need technical, legal, and political review. More importantly, when inspectors lack full access, the sides can disagree about the baseline and later dispute whether commitments have been met.

Therefore, the negotiation is about more than a quantity or storage location. What access would the IAEA receive? How would technical disagreements be resolved? At what stage would sanctions relief begin? Would restrictions return automatically if one side alleged a breach? If these questions remain unclear, even an understanding over the stockpile could prove fragile. Recent reports describe indirect contacts alongside unresolved conditions, while the parties give different public accounts of how close a deal might be. It would be premature to describe the process as either entirely over or on the verge of agreement.

Ultimately, Iran's nuclear doctrine now sits between three broad paths: retaining the stated policy while seeking stronger security assurances, changing some commitments, or announcing a strategic reversal. The first may leave uncertainty intact without a durable agreement; the second could create bargaining space but test confidence; the third could carry substantial regional and international costs. At present, what can be said with confidence is that the domestic debate has become more prominent, not that official policy has changed. In addition, the positions of China and Russia cannot be conclusively inferred from Araghchi's ambiguous phrase. The most useful indicators in the coming weeks will be IAEA access, the shape of contacts with Washington, clear statements from Tehran, and explicit responses from Beijing and Moscow.
