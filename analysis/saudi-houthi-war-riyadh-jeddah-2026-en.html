Saudi-Houthi war: From Riyadh’s PIF tower to the threat against Jeddah

The Riyadh tower attack, a warning against Jeddah and reported Houthi advances toward Aden connect urban security with Red Sea trade, while falling short of proving a sustained blockade.

On November 24, a tower in Riyadh’s financial district was struck for the second time in five days, injuring 11 people, Saudi authorities said. AP initially reported no immediate claim; on November 25, Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree claimed responsibility, but AP said it could not independently verify the claim. That day, the Saudi-led coalition said it struck Hodeidah. The Houthis then claimed attacks on Jeddah and Jizan ports and the Ras Tanura refinery, without evidence of damage; the coalition said four drones targeting Ras Tanura had been intercepted. A Yemeni military official also said Houthi forces were about 60 kilometres from Aden, a figure not independently verified.

Thus, these developments connect three arenas: the security of Riyadh, the ports and shipping routes on the Red Sea, and the balance of power in southern Yemen. Therefore, the Saudi-Houthi war should not be assessed only by counting attacks or threats.

The Saudi-Houthi war and the symbolism of a financial landmark

In fact, the Public Investment Fund tower is more than an office building. PIF sits at the centre of Saudi state investment and is closely associated with the kingdom’s effort to diversify its economy. A strike on a prominent landmark in the capital can carry a political and psychological message even when the direct economic damage is limited: symbolic infrastructure may not be insulated from the conflict.

More importantly, repeated strikes within days create a more demanding test for Riyadh’s security system than an isolated incident. An attacker can use relatively inexpensive and irregular attacks to force defenders to cover more locations. In turn, Saudi Arabia has to prioritize among residents, financial centres, airports, energy facilities and transport links. That is not only a military problem. Security alerts, temporary closures and higher insurance costs may influence businesses even when physical damage remains limited.

Al-Masirah, the Houthi outlet, reported airstrikes near Sanaa airport. However, independent details about the target and responsibility remained limited. A retaliatory strike can signal resolve, but it also risks civilian harm and wider criticism. Therefore, the response should be judged by its effects and ability to contain escalation, not only by its scale.

More broadly, the Saudi-Houthi war also requires separating military objectives from political audiences. By striking economic symbols and then threatening ports, the Houthis can signal to domestic and regional audiences that they can move the costs of war beyond the front line. Saudi Arabia, for its part, emphasizes the defence of sovereignty and infrastructure to prevent vulnerability from being interpreted as a collapse of deterrence. Neither message, by itself, proves that either side has achieved its military objective.

Jeddah, the Red Sea and routes around Hormuz

In particular, the warning against Jeddah matters because the port is a major part of western Saudi Arabia’s commercial and logistical network. However, Jeddah should not be confused with the kingdom’s main Red Sea oil-export terminal.

More importantly, the November 25 claims mean the Jeddah threat was followed by claimed operations, but the damage reports remain unverified. AP said the Houthis offered no evidence, while the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted four drones near Ras Tanura. Shipowners and insurers distinguish between an attack claim, verified damage and a port closure. Moreover, repeated incidents, navigation notices and naval protection affect route risk. A claim alone does not prove sustained disruption.

Moreover, the connection with Bab el-Mandeb is also important. As fighting in southern Yemen moves closer to Aden and the strait, shipping companies may worry about connected insecurity across the Red Sea.

In this context, the Saudi-Houthi war is becoming a test of confidence in routes around Hormuz, rather than proof that those routes have already been lost. Saudi Arabia needs port security, air defence, maritime coordination and uninterrupted operation of the pipeline. Companies, meanwhile, need reliable information and clear incident-response arrangements. If attacks remain sporadic and ports stay open, the economic impact is more likely to take the form of higher costs and caution. If attacks become repeated and coordinated, trade flows and diplomatic pressure could change more substantially.

Can the conflict remain manageable through 2027?

Looking ahead, whether the war continues into 2027 is less a question of prediction than of each side’s capacity to absorb costs. Saudi Arabia has significant financial resources and a broad defence network, yet a prolonged conflict could weigh on budgets, investment plans, urban security and confidence in economic stability. The Houthis also face costs from airstrikes, economic constraints and the demands of governing areas they control. Their durability cannot be inferred solely from their capacity to launch drones or missiles.

More specifically, three indicators will matter in the months ahead: whether attacks on Riyadh recur, whether the warning against Jeddah or other ports becomes an actual operation, and whether the southern front shifts closer to Aden. In addition, Oman, the United Nations and regional actors could help reopen channels of communication. If each incident triggers an immediate and expansive military response, the calculations become harder for both parties. Conversely, clear communication and defined operational limits could preserve room to contain the crisis.

In addition, this analysis connects with the site’s earlier discussion of Bab el-Mandeb shipping after the government-aligned forces’ pullback. That article focused on the maritime corridor; the latest developments show that western Saudi ports and the situation around Aden form part of the same strategic picture.

In conclusion, the Saudi-Houthi war has reached a point where its potential economic and security costs extend beyond Yemen. The Riyadh tower strike, the threat against Jeddah and the reported southern advance are connected pressures, but they are distinct developments. Riyadh’s central challenge is to protect cities and commerce without becoming trapped in escalation. For the Houthis, the challenge is to turn threats into leverage without provoking a wider regional response.

Ultimately, the Saudi-Houthi war should be judged against these indicators. Moreover, the Saudi-Houthi war does not yet amount to a sustained blockade.
