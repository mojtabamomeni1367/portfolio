Israel after Netanyahu remains a political scenario. On November 8, President Isaac Herzog formally asked Gadi Eisenkot to try to form a government. The election had produced no clear path to a parliamentary majority. Consequently, the parties that might join a cabinet and the concessions they might seek remain uncertain. For Gaza, the question is how a prospective government would connect troop withdrawal, Hamas disarmament and the transfer of civilian administration. Each decision can affect the feasibility of the others. The order in which they are made will matter to any negotiations.

## Israel after Netanyahu and the conditions for withdrawal

During the election campaign, Eisenkot supported disarming Hamas before Israeli forces withdrew. In August, Netanyahu likewise said withdrawal from existing positions would require Hamas to disarm completely. That overlap offers a starting point for comparison. However, the two positions leave open what would count as sufficient disarmament and which institution would assess compliance. Those details could determine how a campaign position translates into decisions about troops and territory.

A first observable change could be a different sequence of smaller withdrawals. For example, forces might leave a defined area alongside independent inspection and the transfer of responsibility to a civilian institution. Conversely, a cabinet could defer any repositioning until it considered disarmament complete. In that case, a change of leadership would have little immediate effect on where forces are deployed.

Moreover, withdrawal would become tangible to residents when it changed access to homes, services and roads. A troop reduction could matter militarily while a family still encounters a closed route to a clinic or its neighborhood. Therefore, an assessment needs to examine the date of a withdrawal, the area covered and the rules governing movement. Each tells readers something different about the consequences for people on the ground.

## What would make Hamas disarmament verifiable?

A government in Israel after Netanyahu would face two distinct questions. First, which weapons and facilities must be taken out of Hamas control? Second, who could verify that this has happened? A commitment to disarmament still leaves practical decisions about inventories, records and disputed incidents. Without those details, the parties could issue incompatible accounts of whether the same stage had been completed.

Moreover, the sequence of steps would have different consequences for each side. If Hamas were required to relinquish its military capacity first, it would seek assurances about when the next step would take place. If Israel withdrew first, it would have concerns about weapons returning or military facilities being reused. Consequently, a phased arrangement could narrow the gap if corresponding obligations were clear and disputed claims could be examined.

For Israel after Netanyahu, the response to the first serious security incident would be another test. If every disputed event stopped implementation, a small group could disrupt the process with one action. In contrast, an agreed review period and a channel for urgent communication could give decision makers time to establish responsibility. Such arrangements would also help them relate any response to the available evidence and the scope of the incident.

Meanwhile, the composition of a coalition would shape the prime minister's room for maneuver. A party whose votes keep the cabinet in office might insist on conditions about withdrawal or the authority of monitors. Therefore, the coalition agreement and subsequent cabinet decisions would offer more evidence about policy than statements made on the day the government took office.

## Gaza's civilian administration in Israel after Netanyahu

Running Gaza would require decisions about salaries, municipal services, everyday security and reconstruction. Any proposed institution would have to show where funding came from, whom administrators answered to and how Palestinians would help set priorities. Otherwise, responsibility could be transferred on paper while staff and resources remain unavailable. Furthermore, a governing body needs a clear relationship with municipal employees who already provide services.

A cabinet in Israel after Netanyahu could take different positions on the authority of a Palestinian administration and the role of outside partners. For instance, decisions on equipment entry and staff movement would directly affect its ability to operate. However, the credibility of a Palestinian institution would also depend on local acceptance, financial transparency and residents' access to services. Those factors require decisions by Palestinian actors and other participants as well.

The possible paths range from continuity to a limited trial of new arrangements. First, existing deployments could remain while the tone and diplomatic process changed. Alternatively, a cabinet could try a localized withdrawal and transfer some civilian functions. A third possibility is that coalition negotiations stall or that the parties cannot agree on implementation. Consequently, the proposals remain conditional on the coalition talks and acceptance by other actors.

For Israel after Netanyahu, the strongest indicators would be a written withdrawal schedule, an independent procedure for reviewing security obligations and practical authority for a Palestinian administration to deliver services. If any one remains vague, progress on the other two would be harder to sustain. Therefore, the outcome of Eisenkot's mandate should be judged through observable decisions. Who operates crossings, which areas do troops leave and when do residents regain access to services? Those questions would reveal where a cabinet can move from a change in personnel to a change in policy.

There is also a distinction between an agreement and the ability to carry it out. For example, a published timetable would be more informative if it identified who confirms that each step has occurred. Similarly, a commitment to transfer services would have clearer practical meaning if it named the offices responsible for paying staff and keeping clinics and water systems running. These decisions would help explain why similar public positions might produce different results for the people who live in Gaza.
